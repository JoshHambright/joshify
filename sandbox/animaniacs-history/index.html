I.  The setup

A variety show that happened to be a cartoon

Animaniacs was created by Tom Ruegger and executive-produced by Steven Spielberg — the second collaboration between Amblin Entertainment and Warner Bros. Animation after Tiny Toon Adventures proved, in 1990, that a Saturday-morning show could be made with genuine craft and still sell cereal.

The premise was a piece of studio mythology: Yakko, Wakko and Dot Warner were drawn in the 1930s, found to be so uncontrollable that their cartoons were shelved, and locked in the water tower on the Warner Bros. lot until they escaped in the present day. It is a joke about the studio told inside the studio, and it gave the writers licence to send the Warners anywhere — a Hollywood commissary, the French Revolution, a Beethoven rehearsal — without ever needing continuity.

Crucially, the format was not a sitcom. Each half-hour was a revue: two or three short films, a song, a running gag, sometimes a one-minute blackout. That structure is why the show could hold a Goodfellas parody performed by pigeons next to a segment teaching the state capitals, and why nothing on it ever had to be consistent in tone. Different acts, same bill.

“We’re zany to the max.”

II.  The run

Thirty years, two networks and one very long hiatus

The dates below are a genuine sequence — each one moved the show somewhere it had not been.

1990the precedent

Tiny Toon Adventures lands

Spielberg’s first pass at reviving the Warner cartoon tradition wins an Emmy and, more usefully, establishes a working room of writers — Ruegger, Sherri Stoner, Paul Rugg, Peter Hastings — who would carry straight over. Animaniacs is essentially that room given a bigger stage.

199313 September

Premiere on Fox Kids

The show arrives as Steven Spielberg Presents Animaniacs with an opening title sequence that names its own cast in rhyme and dares you to keep up. Sixty-five episodes are ordered for the first season — a punishing volume that forces the revue format to work, because no single act could carry that many half-hours.

1994the notice

A Peabody Award for season one

The Peabody is a journalism-and-broadcasting honour, not a children’s-television one, and giving it to a cartoon about a mouse trying to conquer the world was the establishment conceding the point: the writing was the achievement. Eight Daytime Emmys followed across the run, several of them for Richard Stone’s music.

19959 September

Defection to Kids’ WB

Warner Bros. launched its own network block and took its own show with it. In the same month, Pinky and the Brain — until then a recurring segment — was spun off into a prime-time series of its own, the only Animaniacs act to graduate to its own show.

199814 November

The finale, at 99 episodes

Five seasons and 274 individual segments in, the series ends — expensive to make, with a full orchestra on the payroll, and aimed at an audience the ratings system kept insisting was smaller than it was. There is no grand send-off episode. The Warners simply stop.

199921 December

Wakko’s Wish, direct to video

A feature-length fairy tale in which the Warners, impoverished in the kingdom of Warnerstock, chase a wishing star. It gathers essentially every recurring character into one story and functions as the farewell the series never got. It reached television on Kids’ WB in February 2000.

202020 November

The tower opens again, on Hulu

Streaming did what syndication never quite managed: it handed the original run to an audience that had grown up and an audience that had not. Hulu ordered a revival, Spielberg returned as executive producer, and — the decisive detail — Rob Paulsen, Jess Harnell, Tress MacNeille and Maurice LaMarche all came back to their own parts.

202317 February

Season three, and the curtain

The revival closes after three seasons, having spent much of its running time openly discussing the fact that it is a revival. Total across both eras: 129 episodes of a show whose central gag was that it should never have been let out in the first place.

III.  The bill

The acts, as they were billed

A rotating company of shorts. Hover a card to bring the cel into register.

Headline act

The Warners

Yakko talks, Wakko eats, Dot is cute — and all three exist to be dropped into someone else’s orderly life and wreck it with relentless politeness. Dot’s full name runs to fourteen words and she will recite all of them.

Voices Rob Paulsen · Jess Harnell · Tress MacNeille

Spun off, 1995

Pinky and the Brain

Two lab mice, one megalomaniac and one idiot, attempt world domination nightly and fail on a technicality. The best-constructed thing on the show: a rigid formula executed with enormous variation, and the only act to earn its own series.

Voices Rob Paulsen · Maurice LaMarche

Old-school

Slappy Squirrel

A retired golden-age cartoon star, permanently annoyed, explaining the mechanics of anvil-based comedy to her nephew Skippy while using them. The show’s vehicle for jokes about cartoons themselves — and about ageing out of your own industry.

Voices Sherri Stoner · Nathan Ruegger

Film-buff act

The Goodfeathers

Three Scorsese pigeons — Squit, Bobby and Pesto — running mob-movie beats on a statue in a park. Pesto’s trigger is any perceived question about anything, which resolves in a beating. Aimed squarely over the heads of children, and landing.

Voices Maurice LaMarche · John Mariano · Chick Vennera

The sincere one

Rita and Runt

A stray cat who sings and a dog who cannot tell she is a cat, looking for a home. Bernadette Peters sang the cat. These shorts are genuinely melancholy and were scored like short films, which on a show this loud reads as a deliberate change of key.

Voices Bernadette Peters · Frank Welker

Silent comedy

Buttons and Mindy

A toddler wanders obliviously into mortal danger; the family dog nearly dies preventing it; the dog is punished. Almost wordless, mechanically perfect, and the same joke every single time — which is the joke.

Voices Frank Welker · Nancy Cartwright

One-joke act

Chicken Boo

A six-foot chicken adopts a disguise, is universally accepted as a man, is unmasked, is chased out of town. Every time. The refusal to develop it beyond that is the entire point, and the theme song says so out loud.

Narration Maurice LaMarche

Interstitial

Good Idea, Bad Idea

Mr. Skullhead demonstrates a reasonable action, then its catastrophic inversion, in under sixty seconds. A blackout gag used as connective tissue — the show’s equivalent of a curtain between acts.

Narration Tom Bodett

On the lot

The studio regulars

Dr. Otto Scratchansniff, the studio psychiatrist assigned to calm the Warners; Hello Nurse, his assistant and a running commentary on the trope she is; Ralph the security guard, permanently outrun; and CEO Thaddeus Plotz, permanently furious.

Voices Rob Paulsen · Tress MacNeille · Frank Welker

IV.  The pit

Somebody paid for a real orchestra

Richard Stone scored the series with a thirty-plus-piece orchestra recording for every episode — a practice that had essentially died out in television animation after the 1960s, when synthesisers and library cues took over. It is the single most expensive decision on the show and the one you hear first.

The idiom is deliberately Carl Stalling: mickey-moused hits, quotations of public-domain classics used as punchlines, a brass section doing pratfalls. Stone, with Steve and Julie Bernstein, won multiple Emmys for it.

Then there are the songs. Randy Rogel wrote the two that outlived the show entirely — both of them list-songs set to public-domain tunes, both of them genuinely used by teachers, and both of them a dare aimed at Rob Paulsen’s diction.

The thing it actually did

Animaniacs is usually credited with writing “for adults too,” which undersells it. The show wrote one script and let it operate on two levels at once: a seven-year-old gets a pigeon falling off a statue, and the person next to them gets a shot-for-shot Scorsese parody. Neither joke is a bonus track for the other. Nothing is winked at.

That approach put the writers in a standing argument with the network’s Standards and Practices department, and a good deal of the show’s reputation rests on lines that survived it — most famously a fingerprint gag whose second reading nobody caught until the episode had aired. The Wheel of Morality exists for the same reason: an educational-content requirement, satisfied by a spinning wheel that dispenses a non-sequitur and moves on.

Its afterlife was built on repetition. Reruns, then home video, then streaming, put the original 99 episodes in front of people who were not born when they aired — and it was that audience, measurable in a way 1998’s was not, that got the tower opened again in 2020.

VI.  The lexicon

Things people still say without knowing why

“Helloooo, Nurse!”
Yakko and Wakko’s response to anything striking. Deployed at sunsets, sandwiches and once at a particularly good chair — the joke being that the phrase escaped its own meaning almost immediately.
“Goodnight, everybody!”
The vaudeville tag Yakko drops on any line that lands too close to an adult reading. It functions as both a punchline and a confession.
“The same thing we do every night…”
Pinky asks what they’re doing tonight. Brain answers: try to take over the world. Said in essentially every one of the ~65 Pinky and the Brain outings, and never once landing the same way.
“Faboo!”
Wakko’s all-purpose superlative, delivered in the Liverpudlian accent Jess Harnell built from Ringo Starr — a Beatles joke sitting quietly inside a children’s cartoon for five years.
“I’m cute!”
Dot’s standing defence and her stated occupation. Full name: Princess Angelina Contessa Louisa Francesca Banana Fanna Bo Besca III. She would like you to call her Dot.
“Wheel of Morality, turn, turn, turn…”
The educational requirement, discharged in eleven seconds by a spinning wheel that produces advice with no connection to anything that just happened. Try it below.
