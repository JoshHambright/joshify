What the hardware actually did

The PS1 look isn't nostalgia filters — it's a specific set of hardware limitations, and each one is reproducible in a shader. The tunnel above runs all five. Toggle them off and it collapses into generic modern 3D.

ARTEFACT 01

Vertex snap

The GTE used fixed-point maths with no sub-pixel precision, so every vertex jumped to the nearest whole pixel. Geometry visibly shivers as the camera moves. Reproduced by rounding ndc.xy to a coarse grid in the vertex shader.

ARTEFACT 02

Affine texture mapping

No perspective correction — textures were interpolated linearly in screen space, so they swim and warp across large polygons. Recreated by premultiplying UVs by w and dividing in the fragment shader, cancelling the GPU's own correction.

ARTEFACT 03

15-bit colour + dither

Only 32 levels per channel. An ordered 4×4 Bayer matrix hid the banding, producing that fine crosshatch in gradients. This one was already in our effects catalogue — the PS1 just gives it a reason.

ARTEFACT 04

No z-buffer

Depth sorting was the programmer's problem, done per-polygon on the CPU, so surfaces flicker and punch through each other at glancing angles. We get this free by drawing tunnel rings back-to-front with depth testing disabled.

ARTEFACT 05

Distance fog

Draw distance was short, so everything faded into coloured haze to hide the clip plane. Here the fog is tinted from the album's extracted accent, which is what ties the tunnel to the artwork.

ARTEFACT 06

240p output

Composite video into a CRT. The whole scene renders to a ~320×180 buffer and scales up with nearest-neighbour — the chunky pixels are the source, not a filter.

This is decision D-011 paying off. We already chose half-resolution rendering because it cuts fragment cost 4× and looks like the lo-fi aesthetic we wanted. It turns out that's also, almost exactly, the PS1's native output. The performance dial, the art direction and the period reference are all the same slider.

Why the two eras fit. Winamp 2 shipped in 1998. N2O shipped in 1998. Milkdrop arrived in 2001. This isn't a mashup of two aesthetics — it's one cultural moment, and building the visualiser and the interface from the same year is what will make the finished object feel authored rather than themed.

Runs in the browser — WebGL2, one draw call, no libraries.
